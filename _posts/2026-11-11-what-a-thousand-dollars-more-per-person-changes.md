If almost no family will ever owe federal estate tax, why should anyone care that the annual gift exclusion went up by $1,000?

Because the annual exclusion was never really about estate tax for most families. It is the cleanest tool the Code offers for moving wealth on purpose, a little at a time, while the person giving it is alive to see it used well. The new numbers are a good reason to revisit that habit.

## The new numbers

On November 5, the IRS released Rev. Proc. 2026-35, its annual inflation adjustments for 2027. Two figures matter most to families and the people who advise them.

First, under section 2503, the first $20,000 of gifts to any one person in calendar year 2027 is not counted as a taxable gift, up from $19,000 in 2026. A married couple who split gifts can give $40,000 to each recipient. The Rev. Proc. is careful on one point: the exclusion covers gifts of present interests, not future interests in property.

Second, the basic exclusion amount under section 2010(c)(3) rises to $15,480,000 for people who die in 2027, up from $15,000,000. The same figure drives the lifetime gift tax credit and the generation skipping transfer exemption. A married couple can shelter $30.96 million between them.

The exclusion for gifts to a spouse who is not a United States citizen rises to $201,000.

## What it means for a family

Consider a couple with three children and five grandchildren. At $40,000 per recipient, they can move $320,000 a year out of their estate without filing to use any of their lifetime exemption. Do that for ten years and the dollars add up, but the real gain is the growth. Every dollar given in 2027 takes its future appreciation with it.

The more important gain is not financial. A gift made while the giver is alive comes with a conversation. A grandparent who pays a semester of tuition or seeds a first brokerage account can watch how the money is handled, teach along the way, and adjust. An inheritance arrives all at once, usually during grief, and with no one left to explain what it was for.

For gifts into a trust, the present interest rule matters. A transfer to a trust is usually a future interest unless the beneficiary holds a real, timely right to withdraw it. Trustees who administer those trusts owe the beneficiaries the notices the trust document requires, every year, on time. A missed notice can turn an excluded gift into a taxable one.

## The honest counterargument

With a basic exclusion near $15.5 million per person, a fair critic will say the annual exclusion is a rounding error for nearly everyone, and that pushing families to give money away early can leave them short later. That second point deserves real weight. A gift is irrevocable, and a client's own long term care, housing, and income needs come first.

## The judgment

The annual exclusion still earns its place, not as a tax maneuver but as a discipline. Give what the plan can truly spare, give it to people ready to receive it, and give it while you can still teach. Steady annual gifts move growth out early and let a family practice the transfer before it becomes final.

That is what wealth transfer with intent looks like. The number changed by $1,000. The duty to plan it thoughtfully did not.
