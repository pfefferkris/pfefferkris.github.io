Is a revocable trust a safe harbor from a settlor's creditors?

It is not, and the Michigan Supreme Court said so plainly on July 20, 2026, in *In re Fowler Estate*. Yet the same opinion held that a 401(k) and a life insurance policy payable to that very trust stayed out of the creditors' reach. The reasoning is worth knowing, because it turns on statutes and not on labels.

## The facts, and the question

In 2018, Jennifer Fowler killed her mother, Helen, and then herself. Helen's estate won a wrongful death judgment against Jennifer's estate, which was insolvent. Jennifer had named her revocable trust as beneficiary of her 401(k) and of a life insurance policy on her own life. The trustee asked the probate court whether those proceeds could be used to satisfy the judgment. The probate court held that the insurance could be reached but the 401(k) could not. The Court of Appeals ruled against the trustee, and the Supreme Court reversed it.

## The rule: the trust is liable, with exceptions

The Court began with the threshold question and ruled against the trust. Under Michigan's Estates and Protected Individuals Code, MCL 700.7506(1)(b) and MCL 700.7605(1), the property of a trust that was revocable at the settlor's death is subject to the settlor's debts when the probate estate falls short. The Court looked to the power Jennifer held at her death, not to the fact that the trust became irrevocable once she died. So the trust, and the proceeds paid into it after her death, were generally open to claims.

Then came the exceptions, which the Legislature wrote into the same section. Under MCL 700.7605(2), "all payments from" a qualifying 401(k) plan are not treated as a revocable trust liable for such claims, so the 401(k) money was protected. Under MCL 700.7605(4), property that would have been safe from creditors if it had been paid "other than to the settlor's estate" stays safe inside the trust. Michigan's insurance statute, MCL 500.2207(2), shields life insurance proceeds from the insured's creditors unless they are paid to the insured or to the insured's executors or administrators. The Court rejected the argument that a trustee is an administrator, and held that a trustee is not excluded from that protection.

## What this means for the family

For a family, the lesson is that the trust did not protect the assets, and it did not expose them either. The statutes decided. Retirement money and life insurance carried their own protection into the trust because the Legislature said they would. A revocable trust used as a will substitute is still liable for the settlor's debts, and a planner who tells a client otherwise has made a promise the law will not keep.

## The honest counterargument

This is one state's reading of one state's statutes, and it is a hard case. The Court itself noted in a footnote that no party raised whether the result changes when the trustee is also the executor or administrator of the estate. Other states word their creditor statutes and their insurance exemptions differently, and some protect less. A decision this helpful in Michigan is not a rule for North Carolina or anywhere else.

## The fiduciary read

My judgment is narrow. Before a planner or trustee tells a family that assets payable to a revocable trust are protected, or exposed, someone should read the enacting state's own text on trust liability for the settlor's debts, and on the exemption that applies to each asset. The answer lives in the statute for that asset in that state.

That is the plain side of fiduciary duty. It means telling a family what the law actually says about their assets, including the parts that are less comforting than the sales conversation, and doing it before the loss and not after.
