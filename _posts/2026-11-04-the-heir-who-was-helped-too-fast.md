Can the fastest service to a grieving family be the very thing that costs them the most?

Under the federal rules for disclaimers, it can. When a parent dies, an advisor's instinct is to move: retitle accounts, sell what needs selling, get the heir settled. That instinct is kind, and it is also how an heir loses a choice he did not know he had.

## The rule: what makes a disclaimer qualified

[Section 2518 of the Internal Revenue Code](https://www.law.cornell.edu/uscode/text/26/2518) lets a person refuse an inheritance and be treated, for estate, gift, and generation skipping transfer tax purposes, as though the interest never passed to him. To get that treatment the refusal must be irrevocable and unqualified, and it must meet four conditions: it is in writing, it is delivered within nine months of the transfer (or of the heir's twenty first birthday, if later), the heir has not accepted the interest or any of its benefits, and the property passes without any direction from the heir to the surviving spouse or to someone other than the heir.

The third condition is where well meaning service goes wrong. [Treasury Regulation 25.2518-2(d)(1)](https://www.law.cornell.edu/cfr/text/26/25.2518-2) says acceptance is "an affirmative act which is consistent with ownership." Using the property counts. Taking dividends, interest, or rents counts. Directing others to act with respect to the property counts. The regulation's own Example 4 makes the point concrete: a beneficiary asks the executor to sell an inherited farm, the executor does, and the beneficiary's later disclaimer of the proceeds fails, because asking for the sale was acceptance of the farm.

## What this means for the family

A David Haughton guest post in [Nerd's Eye View on Kitces.com](https://feeds.kitces.com/~/972340934/0/kitcesnerdseyeview~Hazards-With-Engaging-Heirs-Too-Quickly-How-Proactive-Estate-Settlement-Can-Disrupt-Disclaimers-By-Triggering-Early-Acceptance/) frames the practical danger well. The disclaimer is not only an estate tax tool for the very wealthy. A daughter who inherits a pretax IRA she does not need may prefer that it pass to someone in a lower bracket. A son who inherits after tax assets may want them to reach a person who will get a better basis step up later. If she disclaims in time, the asset goes where the documents send it, and she has used none of her own lifetime exemption. If she accepts first and gives it away afterward, the transfer is her gift, and with an IRA she must pay income tax on the withdrawal before there is anything left to give.

Notice the limit in that last condition. The heir cannot steer the property. She can only say no. The will, the trust, the beneficiary designation, or the custodian's default terms decide who receives it next, so the first job is to read those documents before anyone touches an account.

## The honest counterargument

An advisor's prompt action is not automatically acceptance, and it would be wrong to tell families to freeze. The regulation is explicit that merely taking delivery of an instrument of title is not acceptance, and that an heir is not treated as accepting just because local law vests title in him at death. A fiduciary who is also a beneficiary may take steps to preserve and maintain the property under paragraph (d)(2) without accepting it. Most estates will never involve a disclaimer at all. Moving quickly on a matter that has no tax value in waiting is simply good service.

## The fiduciary read

The judgment I land on is narrow. Before an advisor, a trustee, or a trust officer acts at an heir's direction on an inherited asset, someone should ask one question: is a disclaimer even a possibility here? That costs a short conversation, and the nine month clock is long enough for it. Skipping it can cost a family a choice that no later effort can restore.

That is the quiet side of fiduciary duty. It is not the discipline of doing more for a grieving family. It is the discipline of knowing what they can still choose, and protecting that choice until they have decided.
